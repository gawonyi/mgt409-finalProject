# Brightloaf Bakery: One Brand, Three Kinds of Customers

*Fictional teaching case written for the Hand Up! demo. All companies, people and numbers are invented.*

## Page 1

In September 2026, Dana Okafor, CEO of Brightloaf Bakery, stood in the company's newest store in Riverton and watched the morning line. Office workers in suits grabbed a coffee and a croissant in under two minutes. A few tables away, two parents with strollers were splitting a $14 sourdough loaf and a pot of tea, staying for almost an hour. Near the window, a college student had been nursing a $3 drip coffee since 7 a.m., laptop open, charger plugged into the wall.

"Same store, same menu, three completely different businesses," Okafor said to her head of marketing, Luis Brandt. "And we are trying to make all of them happy with one price list."

**Company background.** Brightloaf was founded in 2014 as a single neighborhood bakery known for slow-fermented sourdough. By 2026 it operated 38 stores in three mid-sized cities. Revenue in fiscal 2025 was $61 million, up 9% from the year before, but operating margin had fallen from 11.2% to 7.4%. The board had asked Okafor for a plan to restore margin to at least 10% within two years, without "turning Brightloaf into just another coffee chain."

**The margin problem.** Brandt's team traced most of the margin decline to two changes. First, flour, butter and labor costs had risen about 14% since 2023, while Brightloaf had raised menu prices only 6%. Second, the average customer now stayed longer: seat turnover at lunch fell from 3.1 to 2.4 customers per seat per hour. Stores were full, but tables were occupied by customers who spent little.

**Competition.** Brightloaf faced two kinds of rivals. National coffee chains (e.g., the fictional "QuickCup") sold coffee and pastries at lower prices with fast service and mobile ordering. Independent artisan bakeries charged 15–25% more than Brightloaf for bread and pastries and positioned themselves as "the real thing." Brightloaf sat in the middle: better bread than QuickCup, faster and cheaper than the artisans.

## Page 2

**Three customer groups.** In spring 2026, Brandt ran a loyalty-app study of 12,400 customers across all 38 stores. The team grouped customers by *why* they came, not by age or income. Three groups stood out (Exhibit 1):

- **Commuters** wanted speed. They visited often, bought coffee and one pastry, and left. Many said they would "pay a bit more to skip the line."
- **Weekend Families** came for the bread and the atmosphere. They visited less often but spent the most per visit and often bought a loaf to take home.
- **Campers** (the team's internal name) used Brightloaf as an office or study room. They visited often, stayed long, and spent the least per hour of seat time.

Brandt noted that age and income did not separate the groups well: "We have 25-year-old Commuters and 25-year-old Campers. The difference is what they need from us that morning."

**Options on the table.** The management team discussed three options:

1. **Raise all prices by 8%.** Simple, but Brandt worried Commuters would switch to QuickCup.
2. **Price by time and need.** A "Fast Lane" mobile pickup counter with a small premium for Commuters; a weekend "Family Table" bundle (loaf + drinks + pastry) for Families; and a "Work Pass" for Campers ($9 per day, includes two drinks and Wi-Fi, with a 3-hour seat limit at lunch).
3. **Close seating at lunch in the 10 busiest stores** and focus on takeaway.

Okafor liked option 2 but worried it would confuse customers and damage the brand: "Is Brightloaf a place to linger or a place to grab and go? Can we be both?"

Brandt's last slide said only: *"Who do we serve best, and what do we stop doing for everyone else?"*
